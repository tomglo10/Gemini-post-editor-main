Key Takeaways for Men Over 50 and Caregivers

  • Routine PSA Screening Is Now Recommended: On November 12, 2026, the American Cancer Society (ACS) issued its first major update since 2010—recommending a PSA blood test every 2 years for average-risk men ages 50 to 69.
  • Earlier Annual Testing for Higher Risk: Black men and men with a strong family history of prostate cancer should start annual PSA screening at age 45.
  • Step-by-Step Confirmation Prevents Unnecessary Biopsies: A single high PSA (3.0 ng/mL or higher under age 70; 6.5 ng/mL or higher at age 70+) should be repeated within 2 weeks to 3 months, followed by a prostate MRI or biomarker test before deciding on a biopsy.
  • Clear Stopping Rules for Older Adults: Routine screening is not recommended at age 75 and older (and is discouraged after age 80 under all circumstances) because harms outweigh benefits later in life.

What Changed in the November 2026 ACS Prostate Cancer Guidelines?

For the first time since 2010, the American Cancer Society directly recommends routine PSA blood testing—every two years for average-risk men ages 50 to 69—rather than leaving testing undecided.

On November 12, 2026, the American Cancer Society (ACS) published updated prostate cancer screening guidelines in CA: A Cancer Journal for Clinicians. Previously, the organization advised men only to discuss whether to be screened with their doctor, without issuing a direct recommendation for regular testing.

Long-term clinical follow-up now shows that regular PSA screening reduces prostate cancer deaths and catches aggressive tumors before they spread. At the same time, modern tools—such as prostate MRI scans and active surveillance—allow doctors to avoid unnecessary biopsies and surgeries for slow-growing tumors.

Plain-Language Definition — Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA):
A protein made by both normal and cancerous cells in the prostate gland; a simple blood draw measures PSA in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL) to flag changes that deserve closer review.

Who Should Get Screened and How Often Under the 2026 Rules?

Screening schedules depend on age, race, family history, and overall health—starting at age 45 for higher-risk men, age 50 for average-risk men, and tapering off after age 70.

According to the American Cancer Society and the National Cancer Institute (NCI), prostate cancer risk rises with age and varies sharply by ancestry and genetics. Notably, the 2026 guideline no longer recommends the digital rectal exam (DRE) as a routine screening test, relying instead on the PSA blood test.

Average-Risk Men (Ages 50–69) PSA blood test every 2 years Applies to men with at least a 10-year life expectancy and no high-risk factors.

What Happens If Your PSA Level Is High?

An elevated PSA does not automatically mean cancer—doctors now repeat the blood test first, then use a prostate MRI or biomarker test before recommending a biopsy.

Non-cancerous conditions—including benign prostate enlargement (BPH), urinary tract infections, recent bike riding, or recent ejaculation—can temporarily push PSA numbers up. Under the November 2026 ACS guideline, abnormal thresholds and follow-up steps are clearly defined:

  1. Know Your Age-Specific PSA Cutoff: A PSA level is considered elevated at 3.0 ng/mL or higher for men younger than 70, and 6.5 ng/mL or higher for men age 70 and older.
  2. Repeat the PSA Test First: Unless PSA is extremely high, your doctor should repeat the blood test within 2 weeks to 3 months to confirm the reading is not a temporary spike.
  3. Use MRI or Biomarker Tests Before Biopsy: If the repeat PSA stays elevated, most men should receive a multiparametric prostate MRI and/or a specialized blood or urine biomarker test to see whether a tissue biopsy is truly needed.
  4. Choose Active Surveillance for Low-Risk Cancer: If a biopsy finds slow-growing, low-risk prostate cancer, the ACS states doctors should offer active surveillance (regular PSA tests, MRIs, and monitoring) first rather than immediate surgery or radiation.

Red Flag Warning Signs: Screening vs. Active Symptoms

Screening guidelines apply only to men who feel well and have no symptoms. Contact your doctor promptly at any age if you develop new urinary or pelvic warning signs:

  • Blood in Urine or Semen: Pink, red, or rust-colored urine or unexplained blood in semen.
  • Sudden Urinary Changes: Pain or burning when urinating, difficulty starting a urine stream, or waking repeatedly at night to urinate.
  • Persistent Lower Back, Hip, or Pelvic Pain: Deep aching in the lower back, hips, or upper thighs that does not improve with rest.

Real-World Scenario: How the 2026 Repeat-PSA and MRI Pathway Avoids an Unnecessary Biopsy

The Situation: Kenneth, age 64, had a routine PSA test as part of his two-year checkup, and his result came back at 3.8 ng/mL—above the 3.0 ng/mL cutoff for men under 70.

The Clue: Instead of rushing straight to a needle biopsy, his urologist followed the 2026 ACS pathway: Kenneth repeated the PSA blood test six weeks later (it measured 3.5 ng/mL) and underwent a non-invasive prostate MRI.

The Outcome: The MRI showed normal age-related prostate enlargement with no suspicious lesions. Kenneth avoided biopsy discomfort and infection risks and returned safely to regular PSA monitoring.

Glossary of Prostate Screening & Diagnostic Terms

Plain-language definitions of clinical terms used in the 2026 ACS screening update (browse more terms in the Aging Health Medical Glossary):

Active Surveillance
Closely monitoring a slow-growing, low-risk prostate cancer with scheduled PSA tests, MRI scans, and occasional biopsies—delaying or avoiding surgery or radiation unless the cancer shows signs of growing.
Digital Rectal Exam (DRE)
A brief physical exam in which a doctor feels the back wall of the prostate gland through the rectum; no longer recommended by the ACS as a routine screening test, though still used if PSA is elevated.
Multiparametric Prostate MRI
A detailed magnetic resonance imaging scan of the prostate that helps doctors tell harmless enlargement apart from areas likely to contain clinically significant cancer before deciding on a biopsy.
Overdiagnosis & Overtreatment
Finding a very slow-growing prostate cancer that would never have caused symptoms or shortened a man's life, which can lead to unneeded surgery or radiation side effects.
Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) Blood Test
A routine blood test measuring the level of PSA protein in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL) to screen for prostate changes in men with no symptoms.

Frequently Asked Questions About the 2026 ACS Prostate Screening Guidelines

1. What is the biggest change in the November 2026 American Cancer Society prostate screening guidelines?

For the first time since 2010, the American Cancer Society directly recommends routine PSA blood testing—every two years for average-risk men ages 50 to 69, and every year starting at age 45 for Black men and men with a strong family history—rather than leaving testing solely up to an open-ended discussion.

2. What PSA level is considered high under the 2026 ACS guidelines?

Under the 2026 guideline, a PSA reading is considered elevated at 3.0 ng/mL or higher for men younger than 70, and at 6.5 ng/mL or higher for men age 70 or older. Any elevated test should be repeated within 2 weeks to 3 months before further testing.

3. Should men age 75 and older still get routine PSA screening?

No. The American Cancer Society does not recommend routine prostate cancer screening at age 75 and older, and discourages screening after age 80 under all circumstances. Exceptionally healthy men ages 75 to 79 with previously rising PSA levels may discuss individualized testing with their physician.

4. Does the 2026 guideline still recommend a digital rectal exam (DRE) for screening?

No. The updated 2026 ACS guideline states that the digital rectal exam is not recommended as a routine screening test for prostate cancer, though a doctor may still perform one as part of a diagnostic evaluation if your PSA level is elevated.

5. Does a high PSA test mean I immediately need a prostate biopsy?

Usually not. Unless your PSA is markedly elevated, the 2026 guidelines recommend repeating the PSA blood test first and then getting a prostate MRI and/or a blood or urine biomarker test to help determine whether a biopsy is necessary.

Preparing for Your Next Preventive Checkup

Whether you are in your 50s, 60s, or 70s, knowing your baseline PSA history and family cancer history puts you in control. Bring your previous PSA lab numbers to your next annual wellness visit and ask your doctor which screening schedule fits your age, risk profile, and long-term health goals.
